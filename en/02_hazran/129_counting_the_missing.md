# Episode 129. Counting the Missing

After those words fell, the space went still for a moment.

Right now, the number lost must be counted first.

Further in, there was only the sound of water boiling. Soon that sound stopped too.
Someone had turned the fire down.

People were moved farther into the inner space. The old machines and a few humans moved quietly, and no one gave orders out loud.

When a machine opened a crate, a person took cloth out of it, and when a person emptied a bowl of water, a machine took it and filled it again.
No words passed between the hands going back and forth.

The work that needed doing split apart.
Those checking on the injured, those sharing out the water, those gathering the children in one place.
And those confirming the number of the people who had just come in.

The number.

The old Hazran man had stopped with his screwdriver set against the metal plate. Two lines had been scratched, and he could not scratch the third.
The old machine waited in front of him, still lowered on its knees.

Of the people from Hazran, those who could walk came to his side one after another. Five of the eight.
Three were leaning against the wall.

The old machine asked in a very low voice.

"Please tell me how many were with you."

The answer didn't come right away.

The old man opened his mouth, then stopped partway. The hand holding the screwdriver trembled over the plate.
He wet his dry lips once and barely got it out.

"Nineteen."

The old machine asked again.

"And the number here now?"

The man looked behind him. He looked at the three leaning against the wall, at the four standing beside him, and at the child.
His fingers folded down one by one.

"Three."

The moment it was said, no one could find the next word.

Sixteen.

No one said it aloud.

The man scratched a third line on the plate. Then his hand stopped.
Thirteen more lines were owed, but he did not scratch them; he laid the screwdriver down on the plate.

A little later someone else spoke. It was the woman holding a child.

"Seven."

"And now?"

"Two."

She shifted the child in her arms. The child's arms went around her neck.
Two.

The next person.

"From nine, two."

The numbers were short. The more those short words piled up, the heavier the space became.

The fourth person could not speak. A mouth opened, then a hand covered it.
The fifth person spoke instead.

"This one's alone. From the start."

The old machine looked at that person for a long time.

"Those who came alone are counted too. From one, one."

The fourth person lowered the hand and nodded.

Three people had spoken, and eight remained.
From thirty-five, seven. With the one who came alone, from thirty-six, eight.

Han Jiwoo clenched her hand and opened it. Clenched it and opened it.
The third time, her hand closed and stayed closed.

Aka had been watching two children sitting in a corner for a long time. The children were listening to the numbers the adults said.
One of them spread small fingers and pretended to count, could not finish, and lowered the hand. The child beside took hold of that hand.
Holding hands, the two of them watched the adults.

Aka went over to the two of them and sat down in front of them. She said nothing.
One child took hold of Aka's sleeve, and Aka did not pull her sleeve away. The small hand gripping the sleeve went slack.
The child had fallen asleep.

Aka did not move, her sleeve still pulled. Her shoulder tilted a little, but she left it that way.

Luhai stood with his shoulders tensed. When the numbers had gone around three times, he swallowed a curse under his breath.

"You do this first every time?"

The tired-faced woman nearby answered.

"Skip it and it lasts longer."

Luhai looked at her.

Her face dry and drawn, the woman went on. Her hands, folding cloth, did not stop.

"The later you find out who's missing, the more you keep looking at the wrong places. You watch the door, you watch the passage, and a single footstep coming a little late has you on your feet again."

She finished folding the cloth and laid it on her knees. She looked at her own hands.

"I did it too."

She said no more.

Luhai shut his mouth. Without meaning to, he looked toward the passage, and then turned his eyes away.

Sion looked at the woman's hands. The hands that had been folding cloth were clenched on her knees.

Sern asked the woman.

"How many of those plates are there?"

The woman looked at Sern.

"Why?"

"I know how to count. If I know how many plates there are, I know how many times this place has counted."

The woman looked at Sern for a long time before she answered.

"They're hung on the inner wall. Go count them."

Sern looked further in, but he did not go.

"I will count them later."

"Right."

The woman said.

"Later."

Just then a younger human further in suddenly cried out.

"Maybe they just haven't gotten here yet."

Not one of the Hazran people. Someone who had been on this layer before them, who looked fifteen or sixteen.
Breathing hard and fast, the young one looked around with a face on the edge of tears.

"There were so many passages. Maybe they got blocked in behind us, maybe they went out some other way…"

The words didn't make it to the end.

The tired-faced woman looked that way. She did not get up; she gripped the cloth on her knees.

The old machine looked that way too. Its uneven optical eye rested a long time on the young one.
Longer than on anyone else.

After a long while, it spoke, very low.

"That may be so."

Several people stopped breathing at once. The young one's face lifted.

The old machine went straight on.

"That is why we count first. To tell apart the names that must be kept to the end from the hopes that must be set down right now."

The young one's face went down. The mouth closed.
Back to the wall, the young one slid down to sit.

Only then did the tired-faced woman get up, go over and sit beside the young one. Without a word, she laid the folded cloth on the young one's knees.

Han Jiwoo looked at the two of them.

There were things from Hazran that had not made it here with them. Machines left behind, a hull left behind, lights left behind.
She did not count them. Instead of counting, she laid her hand on her tool pouch.

It was two screwdrivers short. One given to a child, one given to the old man.
She counted those two.

The old Hazran man picked up the screwdriver again. He scratched a fourth line on the plate.
A fifth.
Slowly, one breath to a line.

Sern spoke to Sion in a very low voice.

"These people are not giving up hope. They are managing, ahead of time, the point at which hope becomes a lie."

Sion could not answer at all.

The old machine was watching the plate beside the man. It did not count the lines as they grew; it only watched.

A sixth.
A seventh.

In their mother's arms, the child was listening to the sound. One scrape for each line.

Sion took the metal plate out of his pocket. Two lines.
He looked at the man's plate. Seven lines. Eight. Nine.
He didn't put his own plate back; he held it in his hand and kept looking.

Ten.
Eleven.

The man's hand stopped at the end of the eleventh line. He set the screwdriver down.
He did not scratch the five lines that were left.

"The rest tomorrow."

His empty hand came down onto his knee.

The old machine nodded. Its head took time to move.

"They will still be here tomorrow."

Beside the young one, the tired-faced woman looked at the plate. Eleven lines.
Her face was that of someone thinking of her own plate. No one asked how many lines it had.

Ater stood at the mouth of the dark exit. He had not looked back once while they counted. Not even the hem of his coat had stirred.
Even so, he had heard all of it, the counting and the scratching of the lines.
He was watching the dark beyond the mouth. No sound came from there.

Further in, the fire was turned up again. The sound of boiling water came again.

Luhai went and sat beside the young one. On the other side.
The tired-faced woman on one side, Luhai on the other. None of the three said a word.
The young one gripped the cloth lying across both knees.

"It was my older brother."

After a long while, the young one said it to no one in particular.

Luhai said nothing. Neither did the woman.
The young one said no more. The hand gripping the cloth loosened.

The old Hazran man heard it. He looked at his plate.
Eleven lines. Five remained.

He counted the five he would scratch tomorrow on five fingers, and folded them down.
