# Episode 129. Counting the Missing

After those words fell,
the entire space seemed to pause for just an instant.

*First, the number of what was lost must be counted.*

Sion repeated the sentence in his head once more.

Since entering Serakion,
he had felt the constant pull of being drawn into some structure.

An order that reads people.
A gaze that classifies.
Corridors that separate.

But the first structure he encountered in this old layer was different.

This wasn't a place that decides where to put people first.
This was a place that confirms who made it here and who didn't—before anything else.

That difference landed with strangely immense weight.

After people were moved further inside,
old machines and several humans began to move quietly.

No one barked orders.
No one called names aloud.

But through it,
the necessary work divided itself naturally.

Those checking the wounded.
Those distributing water.
Those gathering the children to calm them.
And those confirming the count of the newly arrived.

The count.

That came first.

Sion found that fact strangely painful.

Here, before anyone's story,
they had to know how many remained.

Stories come later.
Mourning comes later.
Anger comes later.

The number of those who survived
and the number of those who vanished must be matched first.

It seemed like a habit only those who have lost for a very long time could learn.

Among the people from Hazran,
those able to walk came forward one by one.

One old machine asked in a very low voice.

"Please state the number of people who boarded with you."

The answer didn't come immediately.

One man opened his mouth,
then stopped mid-sentence.

Because the moment he counted,
the missing faces became too vivid.

He wet his dry lips once and barely said.

"Nineteen."

The old machine asked.

"Currently confirmed count."

The man looked behind him.

His eyes counting the surviving faces—
that gaze looked less like confirming people
and more like touching absences, one by one.

"Thirteen."

The moment those words fell,
no one could speak the next word right away.

Six.

No one said it aloud,
but that number stayed in the space far too clearly.

A while later,
similar answers came from other groups.

"Twenty-three to sixteen."
"Nine to five."
"Seven to three."

The numbers were short.
But as those short words accumulated,
the space grew steadily heavier.

Jiwoo clenched and unclenched her hand without realizing.

Loss heard in numbers
has a way of lasting longer than screaming.

Even without saying how anyone died,
how many were missing alone was already terrible enough.

Aka was looking at two children sitting in a corner for a long time.

The children might not yet know precisely
which group they had entered, shrunk from how many to how many.

But the adults know.
That's why they grow quieter.

Luhai stood with his shoulders tensed at first,
but after the numbers went back and forth a few more times,
he finally swallowed a low curse.

"Do you do this first every time?"

That was less a question aimed at anyone
and more a refusal against a method he could never grow accustomed to.

A human woman nearby answered.

"If you don't, it takes longer."

Luhai looked at her.

The woman continued, her face gaunt.

"The later you learn who's missing,
the more people keep looking at the wrong places."
"They watch the door.
Watch the corridor.
Get up again at the sound of a single late footstep."

Those words were quiet,
which made them crueler.

They don't count to kill hope.
They count first so that hope can't tear people apart for longer.

Only then did Sion begin to understand
why the people here wore such composed faces.

Not because they feel nothing.
Because they've been through too much,
and can no longer bear the state of having confirmed nothing.

Then,
a younger human deeper inside suddenly shouted.

"They might just not have arrived yet!"

That one line
scratched sharply across the surface of the quietly settled space.

No one answered right away.

The young survivor was breathing hard,
looking around with a face on the verge of tears.

"There were so many corridors."
"They might have been blocked behind,
or gone out a different way…"

Couldn't finish.

Because the only people remaining in this space
were the ones who had already thought through every one of those possibilities first.

The old machine looked that way.

No denial in its gaze. No pity.
But no cold cruelty either.

After a long while,
it said, very low.

"That is possible."

Several people held their breath at the same time.

But the old machine continued immediately after.

"That is why we count first."
"To distinguish the names to be kept until the end
from the expectations that must be set down right now."

That wasn't comfort.
But it wasn't only cruelty either.

Rather,
it sounded like words necessary to keep from collapsing later.

Hearing that sentence, Jiwoo recalled
the things that couldn't come along from Hazran.

Machines left behind.
Hulls left behind.
Lights left behind.
People left behind.

Every loss had a name.
But in certain moments,
numbers had to come before names.

That might be the first rule that keeps survivors holding on longer.

A while later,
Sern said to Sion in a very low voice.

"These people are not giving up hope."
"They are managing the point where hope becomes a lie."

Sion heard that and couldn't answer.

Instead,
he watched for a long time as the old machine slowly recorded
numbers and names of the newly arrived, one by one.

This is not a place that remembers people beautifully.
It is a place that loses precisely, first.

And probably,
only by doing that
can it truly remember later.
