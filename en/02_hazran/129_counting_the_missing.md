# Episode 129. Counting the Missing

After those words fell,
the whole space seemed to stop, just for a moment.

For now, we must count the lost first.

Sion repeated the sentence to himself once more.

Ever since entering Serakion,
he had felt himself being drawn into some kind of structure.

An order that read people,
eyes that sorted,
passages that split them apart.

But the first structure he had run into inside this old layer was different.

This was not a place that first decided where to put people;
it was a place that first checked who had made it this far and who had not.

The difference struck him as strangely large.

Once the people had been moved farther in,
the old machines and a few humans quietly began to work.

No one shouted orders,
and no one called out names.

And yet, even so,
what needed doing divided itself up naturally.

Some checked on the injured,
some handed out water,
some gathered the children together and calmed them,
and some confirmed the number of those who had just come in.

The number.

That came first.

Sion found that strangely painful.

Here, before anyone's story,
they first had to know how many were left.

Stories came later.
Mourning came later.
Anger came later.

The number who survived
and the number who were gone had to be squared first.

It seemed like a habit only those who had been losing for too long ever learned.

Of the people from Hazran,
a few who could walk came forward one after another.

One of the old machines asked, in a very low voice,

"Please tell me how many rode out with you."

The answer didn't come right away.

A man opened his mouth,
then stopped partway.

Because the moment he counted,
the missing faces became too clear.

He wet his dry lips once and barely got it out.

"Nineteen."

The old machine asked,

"And the number confirmed now."

The man looked behind him.

The look in his eyes as he counted the faces that had survived
seemed not to be confirming people
but touching each absence, one by one.

"Thirteen."

The moment it was said,
no one could find the next words.

Six.

No one said it aloud,
yet the number stayed in the space, all too clear.

A little later,
similar answers came from elsewhere.

"Twenty-three, down to sixteen."
"Nine, down to five."
"Seven, down to three."

The numbers were short.
But the more those short words piled up,
the heavier the space became.

Without meaning to, Han Jiwoo clenched her hand and opened it again.

Loss heard as numbers
had a way of staying longer than a scream.

Even without anyone saying who had died or how,
how many were gone was terrible enough on its own.

Aka had been watching two children sitting in a corner for a long time.

The children might not yet know how far the group they had come in with had shrunk,
not exactly.

But the adults knew.
That was why they grew quieter.

At first Luhai had stood with his shoulders braced,
but after a few more numbers went back and forth, he finally swallowed a low curse.

"You do this first every time?"

It was less a question aimed at anyone
than pushback against a way of doing things he could never get used to.

The human woman nearby answered.

"Skip it and it lasts longer."

Luhai looked at her.

Her face dry and drawn, the woman went on.

"The later you find out who's missing,
the more people keep looking at the wrong places."
"They watch the door,
they watch the passage,
and a single footstep coming a little late has them on their feet again."

She said it quietly,
and that made it crueler.

They didn't count to cut hope off.
They counted first so that hope couldn't go on tearing people apart for longer.

Only then
did Sion feel he understood, a little, why the people here wore such calm faces.

It wasn't numbness.
They had been through so much
that not knowing anything for sure was what they could bear least.

Just then,
a younger human further in suddenly cried out.

"Maybe they just haven't gotten here yet."

That one line
scraped sharply across the quietly settled space.

No one answered right away.

The young survivor was breathing hard,
looking around with a face on the edge of tears.

"There were so many passages."
"They could've been cut off behind us,
or gone out some other way…"

The words didn't make it to the end.

Because everyone left in this space, long before anyone else,
had already thought through every one of those possibilities.

The old machine looked that way.

There was neither denial nor pity in its gaze.
But there was no cold cruelty in it either.

After a long while,
it spoke, very low.

"That may be so."

At that, several people held their breath at once.

But the old machine went straight on.

"That is why we count first."
"To separate the names to be kept to the very end
from the hopes that must be set down right now."

It wasn't comfort.
But it wasn't only cruelty either.

If anything,
it sounded like the words needed to keep from collapsing later.

Listening to that,
Han Jiwoo thought of everything from Hazran that had never made it out with them.

Machines left behind,
a hull left behind,
lights left behind,
people left behind.

Every loss had a name.
But at certain moments,
the number had to come before the name.

That might be the first rule for keeping survivors going longer.

A little later,
Sern spoke to Sion in a very low voice.

"These people are not giving up hope."
"They are managing, in advance, the point at which hope becomes a lie."

Sion heard it and had no answer.

Instead,
he watched for a long time as the old machine slowly wrote down numbers and names, one after another, for the people who had just come in.

This was not a place that remembered people beautifully.
It was a place that first lost them accurately.

And maybe
that was the only way it could truly remember them later.
