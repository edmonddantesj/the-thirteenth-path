# Episode 96. The Time Bought with Blood Grew Thinner

Sion didn't lose the count even once.

He counted as he passed between people, counted as he cleared rubble, counted even as he checked the marks on the tally board. The numbers kept rolling at the back of his head.

The hour was up.

Sion went to the corridor.

Harun was standing pressed against the tilted black body. His right hand braced the wreckage; his left arm stayed tight against his side. The fingers below the binding had a stranger color than before.

"It's been an hour."

"Not yet."

"Sern said an hour…"

"I said not yet."

Sion didn't say any more.

He had seen many times today that saying it wouldn't work. Instead he looked once more at those fingers and turned away. As he turned, he began counting again. The numbers he counted from the end of the hour on were different from the ones he had been counting before.

At the ramp, people were packed in tighter than before.

The fourth line was standing there. Not twenty, more like twenty-some. People kept pressing in from the back until you couldn't tell whether it was a line or a lump.

Luhai stood beside it, looking at the tally board.

"Sion."

"What."

"It's weird."

"What is?"

Luhai pointed at the board. Three marks. The same as before.

"It hasn't gone up for a while."

"I know. It hasn't lifted off yet."

"Not that."

Luhai shook his head. He had a frustrated look.

"The first one going up and the third one going up, they're different."

"Different how?"

"The first one, we just loaded them. The second one, the ship had to be fixed. The third one, that thing had to fall over."

Sion caught what he meant half a beat late.

"So?"

"What goes in keeps going up."

Luhai tapped the board twice with a finger.

"But what comes out is still twenty."

Sion looked at the board.

Three marks. Twenty, twenty, twenty. Looking only at the board, it seemed the same thing had happened three times.

But the first time, people had just walked up. The second had been bought by someone under the ship scraping both hands raw. The third had come from that black thing falling over in the corridor for them, and two had been opened up getting it there.

"A ledger like this doesn't run,"
Luhai said.

"Why not?"

"If what goes in keeps going up and what comes out stays the same, someday you can't pay."

Sion knew where those words came from. This kid had grown up watching days in Hazran's alleys when the ledger lines moved strangely. Now he was looking at this city with those eyes.

"Then what are we supposed to do?"

Luhai didn't answer. His face said that wasn't his job. He only saw when a line was off; what to do about it was someone else's share.

"But I know one thing,"
Luhai said.

"What."

"The price of the next one's already going out."

"What are you talking about?"

"It hasn't lifted off yet, but it's already being spent."

Luhai jerked his chin toward the corridor. Harun was standing there.

"What's going out over there right now is the fourth line's price. And the fourth line isn't even on the board yet."

Sion turned that calculation over once in his head.

Turned over, it was worse. It was a ledger where you paid first and got the goods later. And the goods might never come.

Seorin was at the front of the line, pushing people in.

She hardly spoke. Her hands went first. Turning a shoulder, changing a stretcher's angle, catching the wrist of someone about to fall, shoving baggage away with her foot. There was no empty space between the movements.

Watching it, Sion saw one thing.

Seorin pushed someone who could walk toward the stretchers. That person went half a step, then stopped on their own, and only then did Seorin let go.

It was once. No one saw.

Sion saw.

"Seorin."

"What."

"Just now."

Seorin answered without stopping her hands.

"I know."

Sion didn't know how to say the next thing. Until a while ago, pretending not to see had been right. In the corridor too, and when he took hold of the stretcher handles. But now, if that person had been loaded onto a stretcher, someone else couldn't have been.

"Next time, I'll watch for it."

Only then did Seorin stop her hands.

She looked at Sion briefly. It wasn't an angry face.

"All right."

Sion remembered earlier, when they had split the stretcher handles between them. Back then Seorin hadn't said thanks or that's enough. She had just kept carrying.

This time she said all right. One word more.

Seorin reached for the line again.

But this time it was different from before. Before reaching, she looked over the whole line once.

"This won't work,"
Seorin said.

"What won't?"

"You can't stand them in one long line like this."

"Why not?"

"If the front sways, the back sways with it."

Seorin pointed with her hand at about the middle of the line. From there on, people were holding each other's shoulders.

"From there back, they aren't standing on their own feet. They're standing by leaning on the person in front."

Only then did Sion see it.

It was true. The twenty at the front each stood on their own, but the back stood stuck together like one thing. If one person in front went down, the whole back would topple with them.

"Break them into threes,"
Seorin said.

"Threes?"

"Threes. No touching. Two steps between each."

"That'll take longer to load."

"Falling takes longer."

Sion took that and ran to the back of the line.

Even spreading them two steps apart took time. People didn't want to come apart. Their bodies believed first that staying close was safe.

Sion separated them one by one with his hands. As he did, he said,

"Two steps. Just two steps."

The places he had separated closed up again. He separated them again.

One person wouldn't come apart.

They were gripping the hem of the person in front, and when Sion touched them, they gripped harder.

"Let go."

"No."

"Just two steps."

"Two steps and I can't hold on."

Sion knew what that meant. This person wasn't holding on for fear of falling. They were holding on to the moment when the person in front got on the ship and they didn't.

Sion stood there for a moment.

"Then stand in front."

"What?"

"Go in front, I mean. Then you don't have to let go."

The person looked at Sion once and went forward. Going forward, they let go of the hem.

The hand that had let go had nowhere to go, and hung in the air for a moment. Sion saw it.

It took a long time for the line to break into threes and stand.

In the meantime, the ship didn't lift.

Sion looked at the board again. Still three marks.

Eight runs.

Nothing had gone down. But all this time Harun had kept standing, the side of the man who had given up the binding cloth had kept getting wetter, and the people, two steps apart, were more afraid.

Luhai was right. Only what went in had gone up.

Sion ran to the corridor.

"Harun."

Harun didn't turn around.

"It's been over an hour."

"How much?"

"A lot."

"Exactly."

Sion told him the number he had been counting. Hearing it, Harun held out his left arm for the first time.

"Undo it."

Sion put his hand to the knot. The knot tied earlier didn't come loose easily. He had to dig at it with his fingernails.

The moment it came loose, Harun clenched his teeth.

Blood came. What had been stopped surged out all at once and reached the back of Sion's hand. It was hot.

"Tie it again."

"Now?"

"Now."

"If we leave it a little longer…"

"Then they'll see it from there."

Sion didn't look back. He knew without looking. The line, standing broken into threes, was looking this way.

Sion tied it again. Two turns, then a knot the way he had seen it done earlier. The first time, it didn't work. The second time, it did.

"Done."

Harun tried bending the arm once.

"One hour,"
he said.

"Again?"

"Again."

Sion wiped what was on the back of his hand on his clothes. It didn't wipe off well.

"Harun."

"What."

"How many more times do we do this?"

Harun answered as he raised his hand to the wreckage again.

"Who knows."

"Should I count?"

Only then did Harun look at Sion.

"Don't count that."

Sion had heard those words before. On the first day the man had said the very same thing. That if you counted, you'd also know when the last time would be.

Back then he had thought it was about the number of runs.

"Why?"

"If I know, my hands change."

Sion started to go, then stopped and asked.

"Then can I count?"

"You count."

"Why?"

"Because if you count, I don't have to."

Sion took that answer and turned away.

And he started counting again. What Harun had told him not to count, he didn't. He counted only the hour.
