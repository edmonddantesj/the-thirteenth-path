# Episode 71. The Bazaar That Only Opened After Heat

As the sun began to tip, the scrap market began to bring out a face entirely unlike its daytime one.

At first Sion was briefly unsure it was even the same place.

Daytime Hazran was heat and metal dust and glare before anything else. The wind lashed the tent edges before it reached anyone's face, and the wreckage of the hulks was closer to a threat than to shade. But as the red sun went down and the surfaces of the hot metal plates slowly began to cool, everything that had looked only rough by day began to take on a different role.

Cooling metal made noise. Plates that had swelled all day shrank, and here and there in the alleys they snapped with short, dry cracks. For the first few days Sion had turned his head every time he heard it. Now he did not. In Hazran, dangerous sounds were not that regular.

With the heat gone, the sand that had sat in a thin layer over everything all day sank down. Only once that grey-brown layer had settled did the red-painted tents, the glass fragments, the marks on handles someone had rubbed for years come back up.

The thin metal strips hanging over the tents made a very light sound each time the wind touched them, and the torn heat-shield cloth caught the evening light and glowed like a faint red membrane. What had looked by day like nothing but broken hull frames became, in the hour that crossed into night, posts for hanging lamps, screening walls, markers at the mouths of alleys.

Piles of metal that had been torn apart and hauled off by day, standing up again at evening like the bones of the marketplace.

Watching it, Sion let out a very small breath.

It was not a straight, clean light. It had stitched seams in it and worn grain; it was a light barely pieced together by whatever had survived that day.

But that was why he kept looking longer.

Luhai, as if he had been waiting for that reaction, went ahead and spoke.

"Only seeing the daytime is a loss,"
he said, grinning.
"The real Bazaar doesn't open till the sun's half dead."

Seorin spoke from right beside him.

"You talk like you own the place."

Luhai shrugged.

"I don't own it, but I can be somebody who likes it, right?"

He tossed it off lightly, but oddly it did not sound like empty words.

Sion turned that over, and in the end he asked.

"What's so good about it."

Before answering, Luhai looked around once. His face said, why ask something so obvious.

"Everybody looks at this place like a dump,"
he said.
"But the really precious stuff always hides in places like this."

He jerked his chin at a tent nearby. Inside, things that might have been spices or dried herbs were heaped layer on layer in shallow bowls, and beside them small oil lamps that looked as if they had been melted down from broken glass held red and blue light. A little further in lay decorative plates beaten thin from heat-scorched metal strips, pieces of an old compass, and machine parts that looked like scrap at first glance but that anyone who knew would look at again, price first.

Luhai kept talking.

"In the daytime everybody only wears their hanging-on face. But come evening, the stuff here shows its real face a little."

At that, Sion looked at the marketplace again.

He was right.

By day even the same tent had looked like nothing but a place to trade. Now every alley had a hidden rhythm. Somewhere a sound like a small stringed instrument trembled low, and thin incense and dried fruit, the smell of cooling metal and the smell of dust all mixed together, yet strangely none of them was pushed out entirely.

This was not a place pretending to be beautiful. It seemed, instead, a place where the things that had survived got their own light back, a little at a time, when evening came.

The water seller was at the mouth of the alley.

He held two thin metal cups between his fingers and knocked them together, a dry tick and then another, to call people over. It was less a selling sound than a sound that kept time. The drum behind him was old, but the seam of its lid alone had been replaced with a new one, and a scale had been scratched by hand down its side. Lines marking how far he had sold today, and past which point he would not sell.

Luhai took a cup like a regular.

"How much today."

The water seller told him the price. Luhai's face screwed up at once.

"That's more than yesterday."

"Yesterday the wind didn't blow."

Sion stopped for a moment at that answer. The price had gone up not because of a person but because of the wind. When the wind blew, sand got into the drum's seams; when the seams were replaced, that much was added to the price. Here the weather came and sat down right in the middle of the bargaining.

Seorin asked, short, from beside him.

"Two cups."

The water seller named the price again, and Seorin knocked exactly one notch off it. No more, no less. The water seller gave a short laugh.

"Not your first time in Hazran."

"It is my first time,"
Seorin said.
"It's not my first water drum, though."

The water seller did not answer that. He filled the cups instead. Chipped cups. One side of the rim had broken off long ago, so to drink you had to put your mouth to the opposite side.

Taking the cup, Sion realized for the first time how dried out he had been all day. The water was lukewarm and tasted of iron. Even so, while it went down his throat he could think of nothing else.

"Here, water's the most honest thing there is,"
Luhai said, emptying his cup.
"It can't cheat. You drink it and you know right away."

Handing back the empty cup, Sion held on to those words for a long time.

In a city that put a price on everything, there was one thing that could not lie about its price.

As he turned away, a familiar movement across the alley caught his eye.

A woman was taking a child by the arm and drawing the kid behind her. It was not so much pulling as a hand folding the kid away. Sion knew that hand. On their first day in Hazran, when a child had stared at the outsiders, a mother had dragged the kid by the arm and pushed it behind her back. It was that hand. Then, the mother had said to the child: Don't look. Get tangled up staring at outsiders and your price drops first.

Today that hand was a little late.

The kid saw Sion first, and this time did not look away right away. Only when the mother's hand touched the kid's arm did the child step half a pace back, and even then the kid's head stayed turned this way.

Sion did not raise his hand. If he greeted the kid, that kid's price might really drop.

Instead he met the kid's eyes for just a moment and walked on.

"Somebody you know?"
Luhai asked.

"No,"
Sion said.
"But I saw that kid on the first day."

"Then you do know them."

Sion did not answer. He was not sure that counted as knowing. But he did know that in this city he had begun to see the same people twice. When he first came, everyone had been one mass. Now faces were coming loose from it one by one.

"This way,"
Luhai said.
"Looks like nothing from outside, but the inner alleys are the real thing."

Had they passed the alley he led them into by day, they would have taken it for a passage behind folded tents and gone on. But a few steps further in, a Bazaar far deeper and quieter than the one outside lay hidden.

Overhead, thin cloth and metal netting overlapped and half-covered the sky; red light falling through the gaps mixed with the light of small lamps and mottled the alley floor. Small shops stitched together from scraps of cloth and metal panels pressed in on both sides, and between them water, incense, glass, medicinal herbs, parts, trinkets and old carved plates were all jumbled together.

Looking at it, Sion forgot to speak for a moment.

It was the same place as the scrap market he had seen by day, and at the same time somewhere else entirely.

Luhai shrugged.

"See? I'm not bragging for nothing."

Seorin glanced at him.

"Big talk for somebody who's had one day here."

Luhai did not laugh right away this time. He spoke a little more quietly instead.

"Some things you see better because it's only been a day,"
he said.
"People from outside all see this place as filthy and loud and dangerous. But even in a place like that, there's stuff nobody threw away, stuff they kept to the end."

He looked at a tent at the end of the alley, where half-broken pieces of glass hung like candles.

"I like places like that."

He meant it far more than anyone would have expected.

The moment he heard it, Sion thought he understood a little why Luhai was proud of this place.

Hazran was not a straight, tidy place. But when it came to picking real value back out of things thrown away, it might be more honest than anywhere else.

Seorin heard it too, but this time she did not mock it straight away. She spoke low instead.

"It's a place worth bragging about, all right."

Luhai's eyes went a little wide at that, then he quickly grinned.

"Oh, coming out of your mouth, that one's priced high."

"Don't remember it long."

"Harsh."

Watching the two of them trade lines, Sion suddenly had a feeling that this hour would stay with him longer than he expected.

The red light before the sun was entirely gone, the lamps under stitched tents, one cup of lukewarm water that could not lie about its price, the eyes of the child he had met a second time, alleys where the surviving things got their faces back little by little as evening fell, and Luhai, showing it off like something to be proud of.

Someone who only passed through here could not take this away with them. Thinking that, Sion looked once more.

And in the middle of it all, Sion heard a step very close beside him.

It was Seorin.

A distance he had always known. But today, strangely, he felt it more sharply than in the daytime.

A distance he had passed over without a thought because it was familiar. But under the evening light it felt as if even that familiarity could take on another name.

Sion kept his eyes ahead for no reason.

Seorin said nothing.

Instead, the light of a small glass lamp swaying at the end of the alley brushed past the side of her face for a moment.

Sion saw it, and could not take his eyes away at once.
