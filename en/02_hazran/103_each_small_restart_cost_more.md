# Episode 103. Each Small Restart Cost More

Someone came from the left.

He hadn't come to say anything. He ran up, stopped in front of Sion, and only breathed. Nobody hurried him until his breath came back.

"The screen."

That was all.

Sion ran.

It was the first time today he had taken the way to the left. Until now the left had existed only as a name. The left Zahir had talked about. The left Kael had gone to.

When he got there, it was nothing.

Between collapsed walls there was a passage wide enough for one person, and in front of it a single metal plate stood at a slant. That was the screen. It wasn't even as tall as a man.

Behind it was a second plate. Smaller than the first.

Kael was holding the first plate down with his foot.

Three more people were beside him. One was bracing the bottom of the plate with his shoulder; two were moving debris.

Sion counted the four of them and looked at the passage again.

This was all there was. Two plates and four people.

"How many of you?"

"Three."

"That's it?"

"Four, counting me."

"Before, you said you didn't know how many."

"Got here and it was four."

Sion started to ask what those days had been like, then let it go. If he asked, Kael would answer, and answering took time.

The end of the plate was torn. It had split from the corner inward, a long crack, and the split opened a little wider every time it was pressed.

"You came."

"What happened?"

"This one's going."

"When?"

"Next time they come at it once more."

Sion looked at the second plate.

"Then you just shut that one, right?"

"Then this part's gone."

Kael struck the spot he was standing on twice with his foot.

Only then did Sion see it.

If they shut the second plate, this whole passage would become the other side's. The spot Kael stood on, and the twenty or so paces of space that ran on behind it.

Sion knew what those twenty paces were doing right now.

The back of the line in front of the hull stretched all the way there.

"There are people there."

"I know."

"The back of the line is—"

"I know."

Kael didn't lift his foot.

"That's why I haven't shut it yet."

"Then when do you shut it?"

"Once you've pulled them back."

Only then did Sion look down at his own feet.

This man had been pressing a tearing plate down with his foot, waiting for Sion to come. Because he knew that if he shut it, the people behind would be trapped.

"How many paces do I pull them back?"

"Twenty."

"Then there won't be room for the two paces."

For the first time, Kael properly looked at Sion.

"What two paces?"

"Between groups. I cut them into threes and stand them two paces apart."

"Then stand them close."

"If they're close, when one falls, they all fall."

Kael said nothing to that for a moment.

"That's not mine to decide."

"Then who decides?"

"Whoever made it."

Sion started to move his feet and stopped.

"What about the four of you here?"

"What about us."

"If you shut it, won't the four of you be left on their side too?"

Kael looked at the second plate.

"We shut it after we've crossed over to this side."

"Then who shuts it last?"

"Me."

Sion drew a breath and held it there.

"Go, quick,"
Kael said.
"My foot hurts."

Sion ran.

The way back felt shorter than the way there. It does when you run carrying bad news.

Seorin was in front of the eighth line.

"We have to pull back twenty paces."

Seorin didn't stop her hands as she asked.

"The left?"

"Yes."

"Now?"

"Now."

Only then did Seorin stop her hands. Then she looked at the whole line. The same eyes as when she'd cut it into threes, but this time she looked longer.

"No room for two paces."

"No."

"And they can't stand close."

"No."

Sion waited for what she would say next.

Seorin didn't speak for a long time. The whole while, the line stood as it was, there were still people in the twenty paces at the back, and on the left someone was holding a plate down with his foot.

"If you can't space them out with room,"
Seorin said.

"Yes."

"Space them out with time."

"What does that—"

"Front three go up, count two. Then send the next three."

Sion followed the reasoning.

What the two paces had done was keep the back from touching the front when the front fell. Even if people stood pressed together, delaying the start meant they wouldn't be pressed together on the ramp. The space they stood in got narrower, but the space they moved in didn't.

"But that takes longer."

"It does."

"How much?"

"About twenty counts a line."

Sion knew right away what that number ate.

Twenty counts was twenty counts a line, and with four lines left it was eighty counts. For those eighty counts, the interval of the knocking under the pile would keep growing.

"There's no other way?"

"If there were, I'd have done that."

Sion took that answer and asked once more.

"That took a long time to build, didn't it?"

Seorin didn't answer right away.

"Doesn't it feel like a waste?"

"It does."

"But?"

"If it feels like a waste, you hang on to it, and if you hang on to it, the people back there in those twenty paces don't get out."

Then Seorin said one more thing.

"I made it, so I'm the one who has to throw it away."

With that, she walked toward the back of the line.

"Twenty paces forward!"

People moved. They pressed in from the back.

The twenty paces were gone in no time. What took long was what came after. The people in front didn't give up their space. Not because they didn't want to, but because there was nowhere to give it to.

Sion went to the front and pushed the shoulders of the front row. Each push moved them half a hand's width. He pushed eight times, half a hand at a time, and made four paces.

Meanwhile the people from the twenty paces came in. As they came, nobody asked. Why they had to pull back, what was being thrown away. Because not one person who had asked today had gotten an answer.

The two paces disappeared, and the shoulders pressed together again.

Sion watched it go back to the way it was.

What they had separated one by one stuck back together. Separating them had taken a long time; sticking together took no time at all.

"Count two,"
Seorin said to Sion.

"Okay."

"When I send the front three up, you count two and send the next three."

"Out loud?"

"Out loud."

"Why?"

"So people count with you."

Three went up.

"One. Two."

The next three started.

"One. Two."

Three more.

From about the third group on, a sound came from behind of others counting along. At first it was one or two voices, then five or six. The sound wasn't loud, but it spread through the whole line.

Sion heard it as he counted.

The two paces were gone, yet people weren't pushing each other. Not because there was no room to push, but because while they were counting, nobody moved.

Seorin was hearing it too.

"Do you hear that?"
Sion asked.

"Yeah."

"Why does this work?"

"If people have something to count, they don't push."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

As she sent the next three up, Seorin added one more thing.

"When you push, you have to look ahead. When you count, you don't."

The last two of the eighth line went up.

"Twenty."

The ramp folded. The ship lifted.

Sion went to the board. The marks had become eight.

Sixty-eight, minus twenty. Forty-eight.

Three trips.

And then a sound came from the left. The sound of a metal plate hitting the floor. It came once and was over.

Sion looked that way. He couldn't see it. From here it had never been visible.

It had been shut.

There was no way to confirm whether the four had crossed to this side. Confirming meant running there, and if he ran there, there would be no one here to count.

Sion didn't run.

Beside the short marks, Sion drew the fourth. Then he went to Aka.

"How many?"

Aka counted with her hand against the wall.

She counted a long time. It took longer than before.

"Fifteen."

"It was twelve before."

"Yeah."

"Three more, all at once."

"Yeah."

Sion started to run for the corridor and stopped.

"Aka."

"Yeah."

"The sound isn't getting weaker, is it?"

Aka didn't answer that question right away.

"I don't know about weaker."

"Why not?"

"I only hear the counting."

Sion knew what that answer meant.

This child counted the intervals exactly. How much strength was left, she couldn't count. Nobody could count that.

Sion ran.

He counted as he ran. One, two.

Now he knew what those two bought. One person who might fall. And every time they bought those two counts, down there fifteen was becoming sixteen.
