# Episode 86. The Defense Beyond the Broken Heartline

After the central market line closed, Hazran seemed for a moment to have truly stopped breathing.

The sound of everything collapsing had been loud, but the silence that came after was strangely louder.

Sion was afraid of that short silence. A silence in the middle of a war was not an end; it was closer to the time the next clash took to settle back into place—he felt he knew that now, with his body.

And sure enough, that time was not long.

Only, in between, one thing changed.

The wind began to come in.

The central market line had also been a wall standing across the middle of Hazran for forty years. When it came down, it opened a way straight through from the west to the inside, and the sand that had sat outside all day came pushing in along it, all of it at once.

Sion turned his face away once. Nothing had gotten into his eyes; it grated in the back of his throat when he breathed in.

Zahir took the wind head-on and still did not close his eyes.

"How much is coming in?"
he asked.

Harun answered.

"As long as the sun is up, it keeps coming. When it cools, it settles."

"Then while the sun's up, that's our time."

Sion understood what that meant half a beat late.

Sand was bad for human eyes too. But the other side did not see with eyes. Those lines measured ahead, counted intervals, calculated the collapsed spots, and only then placed the next foot. What happened when a layer of grey-brown dust settled over that calculation—that was what Zahir had just asked.

Harun had reached the same place.

"Earlier, a line in the west stopped twice,"
he said.
"It was not hit and it was not caught, but it stopped."

"Sand?"

"There is nothing else it could be."

For a moment Zahir said nothing.

"Then the open ground is no place to fight."

"Yes."

"Because in tight places the dust doesn't rise."

"Yes."

Listening to that short back-and-forth, Sion felt his back go cold. This city had just cut out its own heart, and it was already counting the dust from the cut as a weapon.

Beyond the collapsed central line, a line that had lost its direction squared its angle again, and the metal sound of it rang low.

"It's coming back around,"
Harun said.

Zahir looked at the collapsed central line a moment longer, then turned his eyes away.

"Bind it again into three inner lines. Don't block wide. Cut it small."

Harun took it at once.

"The first line feeds the passage, the second line bends them, the third line guards the people."

Short words. But by now that was enough for the people of Hazran.

The battlefield had changed completely. The market, which until just now had folded around a single central line, was now broken into small pieces beyond it: the inner alleys, the connecting passages, the narrow spaces in front of the hull sections. The big current was dead; in its place, several small fronts had come alive.

Hazran now had to hold out not as a whole city but on the small organs left inside it.

Harun set his hands moving at once.

For the first time, Sion properly watched him raise a barrier. It was nothing like an Empire defense line.

The first line. Harun laid the frame of a wrecked stall across the mouth of the passage. He did not lay it there to block. The angle he laid it at was knee-high on a person, so whatever walked in would step over it, and whatever rolled in would catch.

"Why not stand this one up?"
one of the shooters asked.

"Stand it up and they smash through,"
Harun said.
"Lay it down and they climb over. Whatever climbs over is open on top."

The second line. He set six thin metal plates hauled from the scrapyard at a slant along both sides of the passage. He did not join the plates. He left gaps about two fingers wide, on purpose.

Sion asked.

"Those gaps."

"For the wind to pass."

"Aren't you blocking it?"

"Block it and the dust doesn't get in."

Only then did Sion understand. This barrier was not raised to stop the enemy; it was raised to draw the sand pushing in from the collapsed central line into the passage. With wind running through the gaps between the plates, the dust inside would keep hanging in the air.

"In here, they will be half a beat late each time,"
Harun said to Zahir.
"Half a beat will not kill them. But it lets Sern go in one more time."

The third line had nothing raised on it at all.

Instead Harun put no one there and pulled up the metal plates laid over the ground. The sand beneath lay bare.

"We leave this so feet sink."

"People sink too,"
Sion said.

"People know before they step."

The words were barely out when Harun drove two low markers into either side of that stretch. Anyone from Hazran would know them; an outsider would not. Sion did not ask what they meant. If he asked, they would tell him, and if they told him, it felt as though he would become one of Hazran's people too.

It did not take long for all three lines to stand. A regular army would have drawn plans, measured materials, gotten the layout approved. Here they moved what had collapsed—laid it down, stood it up, pulled it away.

"How long will this last?"
the shooter asked.

Harun answered without stopping his hands.

"Once."

"Once?"

"From the second time on, they know this shape."

Sion thought that answer explained the whole city. Hazran had never raised a barrier meant to last. It raised something fast that would work once, and once it had worked, raised something else just as fast. Because it was made only of things that carried no price, there was no loss in throwing it away, and because throwing it away did not hurt, they could keep making new ones.

The trouble was that people could not be used that way.

And the one who had chosen, right now, to stand at the end of the first line's passage was Harun.

Zahir called Nasim.

"How many."

Nasim kept his mouth shut a moment before he answered. To Sion, that silence was louder than sound.

"A little over two hundred inside."

"How little."

"Two hundred and eight."

Zahir did not repeat the number. He only nodded once.

Only then did Sion realize he had never before heard people as a number. When he first came into Hazran, this market had been more people than anyone could count. People shouting on the outer level, calling prices, selling water, dragging children by the arm. That was two hundred and eight now.

From the moment people are counted, you can watch them grow fewer.

"How many go on one line?"
Zahir asked.

"The way things are now, twenty,"
Nasim answered.
"Load more and the ship rocks."

"Then ten times."

"Eleven."

Zahir did not correct it this time either. He simply took it.

"Eleven."

Sion knew that word had just become all the time Hazran had left. Hold long enough to load eleven times and it would be enough; fail, and it ended there. Until now the question had been how long they could hold. Now it was how many times.

Nasim suddenly swung around toward the people.

"Don't wear faces like it's over!"
he shouted, sharp.
"It isn't over. If it were over, we wouldn't be running like this!"

It was close to forcing it. But even forcing it was needed. People who had watched something collapse could easily let their hearts go out along with it, if they let their guard slip for even a moment.

Then Nasim added, very low, toward Sion alone.

"Don't tell anyone two hundred and eight."

"Why."

"Count them, and the first thing everyone works out is which number they are."

Sion heard that and could not answer at all.

The one who sets prices knows best what a price does to people.

Zahir looked one last time toward the collapsed central line.

What lay beyond it was not Hazran anymore. A line that had kept this city turning for forty years was buried down there, and sand was beginning to settle thin over it. One more day and no one would know what had been there.

"Now we fight to send them out,"
he said, low.

The moment those words fell, Sion felt the inside of his chest grow heavier once more.

Not a fight to hold. From here on, a fight to send someone, at last, from the inside to the outside—no, beyond Hazran.

Eleven.

Sion did not say the number aloud. He counted it once more, only inside.

And he watched the first line form.

The people stood on their own. No one called them and they stood; no one called them and they cut it off at twenty. The twenty-first person looked at the shoulder of the one in front and stepped back half a step.

Of everything Sion had seen today, that half step frightened him most.

These people were already counting.

The number Nasim had said not to tell anyone—the people here were already counting it with their own feet.

The wind came in once more, and the sand settled on the collapsed central line grew a little thicker.
